ChunkAct: Action Chunking for Agentic Robotics
Authors: Bingxin Xu, Wenrui Bao, Xinxin Liu, Jiawei Zhou, Yuzhang Shang, Emilio Ferrara
First: 2026-10-13T17:59:57+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-13T17:59:57+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Agentic robotics places a large language model (LLM) agent in the robot control loop, where it composes perception, motion, and vision-language-action (VLA) tools under the guidance of a memory and thereby generalizes to new tasks without retraining. This generality comes at a steep price in efficiency. Every primitive requires a round of LLM reasoning over the full interaction history, so the cost of an episode grows with the number of primitives. We find this reasoning largely redundant: once a sub-goal is chosen, its primitives follow a routine already prescribed by memory. We present ChunkAct, which introduces action chunking to agentic robotics. Online, a single call launches a chunk of chained primitives, which executes autonomously and returns control only upon failure. Offline, ChunkAct compiles the memory into these chunks, so execution is memory-free at test time and the planner is reserved for decisions that require reasoning. LLM computation therefore scales with the number of decisions rather than the number of primitives. On LIBERO-Pro, ChunkAct improves task success by 5.0 points over the state of the art in agentic robotics while requiring 2.9x fewer LLM calls, 4.4x fewer input tokens, and 2.3x fewer output tokens.

Authors: Wenxuan Guo, Yingping Liang, Zhaoguo Wang, Ziyuan Li, Angyuan Ma, Zhenyu Wu, Chengkun Wang, Bingyao Yu, Ze Chen, Borui Zhang, Xiuwei Xu, Meng Zhang, Erjin Zhou, Jiwen Lu

Abstract
Scaling expert demonstrations alone does not enable generalist robot policies to improve from deployment. Real-world rollouts contain successes, suboptimal behavior, and failures: imitating every recorded action can reinforce poor behavior, while filtering out failures discards evidence of physical interactions and their consequences. We introduce \textbf{LoopWAM}, a closed-loop world-action model that learns directly from this full quality spectrum, using rollouts from both itself and other deployed policies. Source-dependent noise sampling unifies behavior learning and action-conditioned world modeling, while quality steering and quality-weighted supervision retain full video supervision across qualities and regulate action imitation. Video and action tokens interact throughout parallel denoising, allowing learned consequences to inform action generation directly. On RoboChallenge Table30-V2, three deployment-and-training rounds raise success from 23.67\% to 43.33\%, a 19.7-percentage-point gain, with a final score of 56.80. LoopWAM also achieves 94.5\% average success on RoboTwin~2.0 and 49.5\% overall success on RoboCasa365 without additional embodied pretraining, obtaining the best overall results among the compared methods across all three benchmarks. On challenging shirt folding task, two rounds of deployment-driven improvement after single-task post-training raise success from 13.20\% to 55.56\%. These results demonstrate closed-loop improvement from mixed-quality experience under both multi-task learning and single-task post-training. \href{https://loop-wam.github.io/}{\textcolor{orange}{Project Page}}

Cepheid: Decoupling Scale from Structure for Cross-Family Motion Completion
Authors: Chih-Hai Su, Tsung-Wei Ke, Yu-Lun Liu
First: 2026-10-13T17:59:53+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-13T17:59:53+00:00
Comments: 31 pages. Project page: https://su-terry.github.io/cepheid-website/

Abstract
Sparse-to-dense motion completion recovers dense mesh motion from sparse vertex displacements. Residual priors trained on one geometry family frequently fail to beat parameter-free harmonic extension off-family. Moreover, cross-family training meets a scaling barrier: residual amplitude varies by more than an order of magnitude across geometry families and collapses as observations become denser. Since this scale depends on unknown target motion, it cannot be measured directly at test time. We decouple residual structure from physical scale by learning a dimensionless prior, estimating amplitude from observables and a lightweight correction fitted only on training sources. Our unified model surpasses harmonic extension across all evaluated families, including unseen cloth and legged bodies, without skeletons, registration, or cross-mesh correspondence. The same prior applies without retraining to point-tracked animal video and a robot grasping a soft body. Project page: https://su-terry.github.io/cepheid-website/

AesCode: Aesthetic Code Generation with Decoupled Cross-Modal Rewards
Authors: Xin Gao, Yifan Yang, Xin Li, Pan Wang, Yan Li, Muzhao Tian, Mingxi Cheng, Bei Liu, Kai Qiu, Qi Dai, Dong Chen, Yue Dong, Leandros Tassiulas, Rex Ying, Ji Li, Chong Luo

Abstract
Generating information-rich visual artifacts such as slides, posters, and dashboards as code is appealing because the outputs are structured, editable, and verifiable. Yet code models cannot see how their choices of layout and color come together on the canvas, often limiting spatial planning and aesthetic quality. Image generators excel at visual design but can misrender requested content, making their outputs unreliable targets for direct reproduction. We therefore introduce AesCode, which generates code grounded in the content prompt while using an image generated from the same prompt as an aesthetic guidance. To do so, AesCode represents the target as a design graph that describes the whole canvas and makes each property attributable. From this graph, we derive complementary cross-modal rewards: deterministic verifiers score properties parsable from code and rendering, while a VLM judge assesses the non-parsable ones with a sample-specific Visual Graph Rubric (VGR) tied to graph elements and relations. We optimize these decoupled rewards through reinforcement learning with per-channel normalization to jointly improve spatial planning, aesthetic quality, and verifiable correctness. To evaluate this setting, we build a benchmark that scores verifiable correctness and visual quality separately, with visual scores highly consistent with human judgments. We find that reference images substantially improve the visual quality of code generated by existing models, and ablations show that AesCode benefits from references both as model input and as a source of visual rewards. Relative to same-scale Qwen3-VL baselines given the same references, AesCode-8B and AesCode-32B improve visual quality by 31.1 and 22.4 percentage points, reaching scores comparable to those of closed-source frontier models such as GPT-5.5 and Claude Opus 4.8.

Interaction Latents as Actions for Contact-Rich Manipulation
Authors: Yuhang Zheng, Yujie Zang, Yuchen Liu, Yupeng Zheng, Zhennan Jiang, Zebin Xing, Shuai Tian, Songen Gu, Jingxiang Guo, Chen Gao, Zhongpu Xia, Shuicheng Yan
First: 2026-10-13T17:59:16+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-13T17:59:16+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2 · Project1
Abstract
Contact-rich manipulation requires robots to coordinate not only where to move, but also how contact and force should evolve throughout an interaction. Yet most robot policies represent actions primarily as motion trajectories, using tactile signals as inputs or auxiliary prediction targets without explicitly encoding contact dynamics in the action representation. We propose ILA (Interaction Latents as Actions), which uses decodable interaction latents that jointly capture the evolution of motion, contact, and force application as policy action units, providing a shared representation for action generation, evaluation, and offline improvement. We learn these latents through visually conditioned joint action-tactile reconstruction with a time-conditioned implicit decoder. Using them as flow-matching generation targets supports post-training of pretrained vision-language-action models (VLAs) and world action models (WAMs), as well as training flow-matching policies from scratch. A shared decoder maps generated latents to executable actions. For offline improvement, we learn a tactile-conditioned expert reference model from interaction latents encoded from expert demonstrations. The resulting expert-consistency rewards guide weighted learning from successful rollouts, while observation-compatible expert references guide correction of low-reward predictions from failed rollouts. Across three policy families, ILA improves success rates averaged over four real-world tasks by 21.0-36.5 percentage points relative to tactile-conditioned direct-action baselines. Ablations highlight the benefit of encoding contact evolution in action targets beyond tactile conditioning, while the same latent space supports expert-guided offline improvement. Project website: https://mrsecant.github.io/ILA/.

Abstract
Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) relies on costly model-generated responses, motivating larger rollout batches and square-root learning-rate scaling. Reported to approximately preserve progress per sample with one optimizer step per rollout, this rule is less understood in practical multi-step training. We develop a two-clock framework that separates the samples needed to make progress from those consumed before collapse. Under a local quadratic model, the square-root rule preserves first-order drift per sample but not progress: larger batches take fewer, longer updates and lose more to curvature, yielding a learning-rate-dependent critical batch beyond which sample efficiency degrades. A drift-threshold hypothesis predicts a complementary failure clock whose sample count remains approximately invariant along the square-root rule. Controlled GRPO experiments on Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B across a $16\times$ batch range support both predictions. Ratio clipping delays failure, especially for larger batches, but the progress gap persists without it. Widening the clip trades stability for a transient speed gain. The progress gap reappears on another dataset, a general-purpose checkpoint, and a 7B model. Finally, we use the two-clock framework to derive a simple multi-step RLVR schedule: start below the critical batch and switch to a larger batch before failure. Our code is available at: https://github.com/WinstonLiyt/two-clocks-rlvr.

Abstract
Expert demonstrations show how to complete contact-rich tasks, but provide limited coverage of how interaction forces respond to different actions. Task-agnostic play provides broader experience of contact dynamics without specifying task goals. To fully leverage these two types of data, we introduce ForceWAM, a force world action model that decouples knowledge of task-compatible interaction outcomes from knowledge of how local actions change contact. A world model learns from successful demonstrations to jointly predict future video and six-axis force/torque trajectories. Together, these futures describe the expected evolution of motion and contact during the task. A separate inverse dynamics model (IDM) learns from both task and play, mapping current observations and recorded visual-wrench futures to their associated robot actions. Play data thus provide knowledge of force responses for goal-directed execution. At deployment, the IDM reactively translates generated futures into actions, reusing them with fresh wrench feedback between world-model updates. Across real-robot force-regulated sliding, box flipping with and without disturbances, and ring placement, ForceWAM achieves $85$-$95\%$ mean success, surpassing world-modeling and reactive baselines. Our experiments further show that adding task-agnostic play data improves success by $15$-$45$ percentage points, demonstrating its value for learning contact-rich execution. Project page and code are available at https://force-wam.github.io/.

Abstract
Multi-objective molecular optimization is a critical process in drug discovery that aims to modify a given molecule to simultaneously improve multiple properties while maintaining a similar overall structure. This process often requires iterative modifications guided by computational or experimental feedback. Recent large language model (LLM)-based methods have begun to explore this iterative approach; however, these methods are not explicitly trained to leverage such feedback, while existing approaches primarily focus on single-turn molecular optimization. To address this gap, we introduce Multi-turn Feedback Guided (MFG) molecular optimization, a reinforcement learning framework that trains an LLM to progressively optimize molecules using feedback from previous turns. MFG introduces a feedback vector capturing both property improvements and source molecule similarity, alongside a feedback-aligned reward function that promotes progressive improvement across turns. We apply MFG over $\text{GeLLM}^{3}\text{O}$, a state-of-the-art LLM model for multi-objective molecular optimization, evaluate $\text{GeLLM}^{3}\text{O-MFG}$ on ten multi-objective molecular optimization tasks. The results demonstrate that $\text{GeLLM}^{3}\text{O-MFG}$ achieves the highest average similarity-constrained success rate ($81.50%$), outperforming the base model $\text{GeLLM}^{3}\text{O}$ ($70.81%$).

Abstract
Collaborative planning under operational constraints is essential for heterogeneous robot teams in large-scale missions. Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs) provide rapid area coverage but are limited by onboard energy, while Unmanned Ground Vehicles (UGVs) can serve as mobile recharging stations yet are constrained by road-network traversability. Effective collaboration must enforce operational constraints during execution while enabling parallel, coordinated actions. Masked Diffusion Models (MDMs) have shown promise through token unmasking but have two key limitations: entropy-guided scheduling assumes distributional alignment and reliance on differentiable constraints for diffusion-time optimization. We propose Masked Error-bounded Neuro-Symbolic Diffusion (MEND), which formulates collaborative planning as a conditional masked generative process to generate context-aware action sequences for UAV-UGV teams. MEND introduces a neuro-symbolic diffusion paradigm that constrains conditional masked diffusion policies at execution by reweighting the learned action distribution with a logic-induced admissibility indicator, thereby enforcing compliance with operational constraints. MEND introduces an error-bounded inference scheduling at execution that constructs the unmasking schedule by selecting positions with minimal predictive entropy over the constraint-aware action distribution, enhancing collaborative planning optimality under operational constraints. Extensive experimental results in simulation, on real-world maps, and with physical robot teams demonstrate that MEND advances UAV-UGV collaborative planning under operational constraints.

Abstract

Abstract
Continual reinforcement learning must balance retention with adaptation, yet many methods still rely on \emph{single-model preservation}, committing to one evolving policy as the main reusable solution across tasks. Even when a previously successful policy is retained, it may no longer provide a reliable starting point for rapid adaptation after interference, reflecting a form of \emph{loss of plasticity} that single-policy preservation cannot address. Inspired by quality-diversity methods, we introduce \textsc{TeLAPA} (Transfer-Enabled Latent-Aligned Policy Archives), a continual RL framework that organizes behaviorally diverse policy neighborhoods into per-task archives and maintains a shared latent space so that archived policies remain comparable and reusable under non-stationary drift. This perspective shifts continual RL from retaining isolated solutions to maintaining \emph{skill-aligned neighborhoods} with competent and behaviorally related policies that support future relearning. In our MiniGrid CL setting, \textsc{TeLAPA} learns more tasks successfully, recovers competence faster on revisited tasks after interference, and retains higher performance across a sequence of tasks. Our analyses show that source-optimal policies are often not transfer-optimal, even within a local competent neighborhood, and that effective reuse depends on retaining and selecting among multiple nearby alternatives rather than collapsing them to one representative. Together, these results reframe continual RL around reusable and competent policy neighborhoods, providing a route beyond single-model preservation toward more plastic lifelong agents.

Abstract
In cooperative Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL), agents must learn to coordinate their actions to achieve shared objectives. A key indicator of effective coordination is behavioral diversity, i.e., the agents' potential to enact distinct yet complementary control policies given the same set of observations. Although behavioral diversity can enable role specialization and richer joint behaviors, its appropriate level is highly context- and task-dependent and often costly to tune. We propose Adaptive Diversity Control (ADiCo), a two-timescale framework that adapts behavioral diversity online during training. On a fast timescale, Diversity Control (DiCo) regulates system diversity toward a target value. On a slower timescale, an Extremum Seeking Controller (ESC) adjusts this target using only mean episodic returns, without requiring an explicit model or analytical gradient of the diversity-performance landscape. We evaluate ADiCo on six configurations across two cooperative MARL tasks, where it maintains task-appropriate diversity levels: low diversity when homogeneous policies are beneficial and higher diversity when heterogeneous policies are required. Through this adaptation, ADiCo matches or exceeds the mean performance of DiCo (best grid-searched diversity target) and Unconstrained DiCo (diversity allowed to emerge without target regulation). These results demonstrate that incorporating classical feedback control into MARL training enables effective coordination by providing a systematic alternative to task-specific manual selection of the diversity target.

Abstract
Frontier language-model agents can now improve robots on their own: they write control code, test it, and keep what works as skills and memories for the next task. We introduce SEAR (Self-Evolving Agentic Robotics), a framework and benchmark built around two questions. Is the agent really learning, or just trying more often? And does what it learns still help on new tasks, in a new simulator, and on a real robot? The framework runs an agent in three modes: it writes code that becomes the policy (code-as-policy), acts live in the control loop (agent-as-policy), or works as a researcher that fine-tunes a vision-language-action model (auto-research). Across seven frontier language models, 330 simulated and five physical tasks, and over four years of agent runtime, we highlight three findings. First, the learning curve of a robotic agent depends both on the overall time budget and the task difficulty relative to model capability. Harder tasks make longer sessions worthwhile; on easier tasks, re-rolling short attempts does just as well. Second, control code and shared routines transfer across tasks and simulators, while task-specific solutions do not. On a real robot, we observe a performance gap between agent-designed practice and novel human-designed scenes. Third, different models evolve more effectively in different ways. Some do best acting live, others writing code first. And a text-only model keeps pace with a comparable multimodal one: perception helps with error assessment, but agentic reasoning and tool use can matter more. Abstract
Long-term robot autonomy depends on more than effective action generation: a policy must recognize when its behavior is no longer sufficiently supported and use assistance to improve. We introduce Equilibrium Energy Control (EqC), a framework for introspective lifelong robot learning built around an explicit conditional energy landscape. EqC casts generative action refinement as equilibrium-seeking optimization, while the geometry of the refinement process reveals whether the generated behavior remains consistent with the learned landscape. This competence signal enables Energy-Gated DAgger, where persistent energy-consistency violations trigger expert takeover and the resulting recovery trajectories are aggregated for subsequent policy updates. EqC integrates naturally into both Diffusion Transformer policies and flow-matching-based vision-language-action (VLA) policies. Across simulation and real-world bimanual manipulation, EqC matches or improves control performance and robustness while enabling timely intervention near the policy's competence boundary. A real-world study further shows how these interventions progressively expand the range of situations handled autonomously, providing a practical path from static generative controllers toward self-improving robot systems.

Abstract
A robot needs a world model that predicts how its actions will change the scene and a policy that chooses those actions. Models are usually built and evaluated for only one of these roles, and every robot records its actions in its own format. We ask whether one robot world model can serve both roles across different bodies, and what makes this work. We present GreenWAM, a world foundation model for robotics, together with the recipe that produces it: (1) a shared anatomical action space that describes 20 robot families, from bimanual arms to dexterous humanoids, in common joint coordinates; (2) joint training on these families in visual, action-conditioned and world-action modes; and (3) policies that split an action expert off the generator. As a world model, GreenWAM attains top-tier trajectory, depth and 3D accuracy on WorldArena 1.0, ahead of every world-action model and video generator in our comparison. As a policy, it scores among the top entries of the RoboTwin 2.0 leaderboard, reaches the highest zero-shot LIBERO-plus success among the compared methods and is competitive with dedicated vision-language-action and world-action policies on CALVIN and LIBERO. We further adapt GreenWAM to a real humanoid, where it succeeds more often than policies adapted from $π_{0.5}$ and from Cosmos 3 Edge, and distill it into a single-step policy for real-time control. Controlled experiments yield three findings: robot specialization improves world prediction and control together; an action expert helps when split off at the start of adaptation; and keeping world-model modes during policy adaptation preserves robustness to scene changes.

Abstract
Test-time scaling for large language models (LLMs) can involve generating multiple candidate solutions for selection or revising an earlier attempt. To guide selection and revision, we need a verifier that can estimate candidate correctness and provide evidence of failure. In competitive programming, however, official judging systems provide authoritative verdicts under finite submission budgets, without revealing failure explanations or failing inputs. To provide informative feedback without consuming official submissions, we formulate a task in which a model judges and diagnoses a candidate program using only the problem statement and source code, with generated counterexamples serving as checkable evidence. To evaluate this task, we build USACO-JUDGE from the USACO 2026 contests, using accepted and unaccepted programs written by human experts and multiple LLMs. We then train ARBITER, a 35B mixture-of-experts (MoE) agentic verifier, with reinforcement learning in a generic execution sandbox. Training raises counterexample success on incorrect programs from 40.61% to 62.67% and mean binary verdict accuracy on USACO-JUDGE from 71.41% to 87.41%, approaching frontier verification performance. Beyond verification, ARBITER outperforms baselines in both code selection and refinement. In an IOI 2026 case study, ARBITER-guided refinement raises GPT-OSS-120B's expected score above the gold-medal threshold under the official submission limit.

Abstract
Vision-language model (VLM) agents can control robots through visual feedback and action primitives, but repeated LLM calls and redundant observations incur substantial token overhead. We introduce PyRUA-Lean, an interactive code-execution framework in which the agent composes robot primitives, including vision-language-action (VLA) policies, into Python cells that check and retry locally and return only the images and state they request. Across 700 simulated task instances from LIBERO-PRO, RoboTwin 2.0, and RoboCasa365, it raises success from 63.1% to 71.7% over tool calling with the same GPT-6 Astra planner and primitives, using 49% fewer LLM calls and 65% fewer input tokens on jointly solved instances. On RoboDojo, without any VLA policy or privileged information, it reaches 51.6% average success rate (Score 58.2), 19.4 points above the top entry of the public leaderboard. Based on the PyRUA-Lean framework, we train PyRUA-Lean Coder v0.1-4B, an open 4B model, with GRPO on 45 LIBERO-PRO instances; it improves from 18.8% to 44.1% on unseen task instances, surpassing the Qwen3.5-27B base model.

Abstract

Abstract

Abstract
Evaluating robot policies before deployment typically requires producing new trajectories through real-world execution, simulators, or learned world models. These rollout-based approaches impose infrastructure requirements; simulator and world-model evaluators additionally face prediction errors that can compound over the evaluation horizon. In this paper, we study off-policy evaluation as an alternative to rollout-based robot-policy evaluation methods. Specifically, we introduce RoboFQE, an evaluator that estimates the discounted expected return of a target policy from offline robot trajectories without estimating a behavior-policy density, combining fitted Q-evaluation with a pretrained vision foundation model and learned terminal success annotation to bridge the gap between standard OPE benchmarks and continuous-control visual manipulation. Controlled experiments across simulated and real-world manipulation tasks show that RoboFQE produces accurate discounted-return estimates and preserves policy rankings even on long-horizon manipulation tasks where video world-model evaluators are degraded by autoregressive prediction errors. Ablation experiments show that pretrained vision encoders substantially outperform from-scratch alternatives, particularly on long-horizon tasks, and that learned terminal success annotation enables evaluation without ground-truth reward labels with only slight reduction of accuracy.

Abstract
Action segmentation supports intention estimation and execution monitoring in assisted teleoperation, but robot demonstrations are costly to collect. We propose an uncertainty-aware framework that transfers temporal knowledge from human to robot demonstrations through MS-TCN++~\cite{li2020mstcnpp} pretraining and fine-tuning, reliability-aware sequence correction, and class-conditional conformal prediction. Across 22 toy-car assembly action classes, fine-tuning with 9 robot demonstrations improves test Edit score from 55.23 to 72.51 and segmental F1@50 from 10.97 to 16.86. Unrestricted correction reduces Edit to 67.86, while selective acceptance maintains 73.45, primarily safeguarding against harmful relabeling. Conformal prediction achieves 95.38\% empirical coverage at a 93\% target, but prediction sets average 17.41 of 22 classes. These findings support human temporal pretraining while revealing persistent challenges in reliable correction and informative uncertainty quantification with limited robot supervision.
