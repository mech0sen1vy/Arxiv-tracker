Coordination Matters: Process-Aware Evaluation of Cooperative Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning
Authors: Maria Ana Cardei, Matthew Landers, Afsaneh Doryab
First: 2026-05-07T16:50:53+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-16T17:58:51+00:00
Comments: Under review
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Cooperative multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) benchmarks commonly emphasize aggregate outcomes such as return, success rate, or completion time. While essential, these metrics can obscure how agents coordinate and fail to distinguish policies that achieve similar outcomes through different interaction processes. We propose a process-aware evaluation framework that complements aggregate performance with diagnostics of interference, coordinated task completion, participation, and coordination efficiency. We evaluate the framework in STAT, a controlled spatial task-allocation testbed, and examine its broader applicability in two established cooperative MARL benchmarks. Across diverse value-based and actor-critic methods, we find policies with nearly identical return can exhibit substantial differences in coordination behavior. Controlled scaling in STAT reveals distinct process signatures for spatial, task, and agent scaling, while a reward-shaping ablation shows that explicitly penalizing assignment conflicts can alter coordination behavior with little change in task return. Results in the established benchmarks further show that the framework remains informative under different coordination mechanisms. These findings support process-aware evaluation as a complement to outcome-based benchmarking. The framework provides a common structure for selecting and interpreting environment-specific diagnostics that characterize not only what cooperative policies achieve, but how they coordinate to achieve it.

Abstract
Every robot already carries high-resolution contact sensors: its own joints. Legged robots and humanoids exploit them to walk, run, and track whole-body motions without vision, but there the contacts are large and forceful. Is joint sensing also enough for precise, contact-rich assembly, where the robot must find a millimeter-scale opening, mesh gear teeth, or catch a thread through light contact? We study this question by training blind assembly policies that observe only a short history of joint positions, velocities, and their own actions, without visual, force, torque, or tactile sensing. The poses of both the fixture and the part are unknown to the policy, and the grasp is randomized substantially. The policy must therefore assemble the parts through contact, sensed exclusively through its own proprioception. Trained with reinforcement learning entirely in simulation over randomized parts, grasps, and contacts, such policies learn peg insertion, gear meshing, and nut threading. The peg insertion policy transfers zero-shot to the real world and inserts pegs of different shapes and sizes in 73% of trials. Controlled comparisons with matched visual policies in simulation show that blind policies generalize far better to unseen part shapes and sizes, though they can be slower. Training such policies needs thousands of different assemblies, each simulated with its clearance intact, so we also release an open-source generator that builds them procedurally from exact convex pieces for MuJoCo; it is more accurate and faster than convex decomposition or MuJoCo's signed-distance collision. Videos and interactive simulations are at https://younghyopark.me/blind-proprioceptive-assembly/.

Abstract
Diffusion has emerged as a powerful framework for generative modeling, achieving remarkable success in applications such as image and audio synthesis. Enlightened by this progress, we propose a novel diffusion-based approach for symbolic regression. We construct a random mask-based diffusion and denoising process to generate diverse and high-quality equations. We integrate this generative processes with a token-wise Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) method to conduct efficient reinforcement learning on the given measurement dataset. In addition, we introduce a long short-term risk-seeking policy to expand the pool of top-performing candidates, further enhancing performance. Extensive experiments and ablation studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of our approach.

Self-Building Labs
Authors: Jiaru Bai, Yuchi Zhao, Han Hao, Miroslav Bogdanovic, Yang Cao, Varinia Bernales, Alán Aspuru-Guzik

Abstract
Self-driving laboratories (SDLs) accelerate scientific discovery by automating experimental execution and optimization. AI agents can increasingly propose new experiments, but existing SDLs can only run the procedures they were built for. Adapting them to a new experiment still takes extensive manual engineering, which makes laboratory flexibility the bottleneck for scaling discovery. We introduce the self-building laboratory (SBL), which designs, configures, and operates its own laboratory setup given a high-level scientific goal. SBL starts from whatever already exists, from an empty room to a working SDL, and builds the missing capabilities: the lab layout, its digital twin, robot skills, and experimental workflows. We demonstrate the system working across different robots and setups, scaling from a single workstation to laboratories with several robots working together. We validate SBL's end-to-end capabilities through a real-world quantum-dot synthesis experiment. By shifting the role of human researchers from manually configuring and programming device automation to specifying scientific objectives, SBL makes scientific automation more accessible, flexible, and scalable. Project website: https://self-building-labs.github.io/

Abstract
Designing RNA sequences that satisfy target tertiary-structure objectives is an important problem in RNA engineering, with broad applications in synthetic biology and therapeutics. Existing 3D RNA design methods have made substantial progress, but they largely treat the target fold as a geometry-only objective. This is incomplete for RNA, whose functional folds are specified not only by tertiary geometry but also by secondary base-pairing topology. We therefore formulate RNA inverse design as a topology-and-geometry co-design problem and propose TANGO, a multi-objective multi-agent reinforcement learning framework for this task. TANGO adopts a staged curriculum: it first learns a topology-aware policy from secondary-structure targets, then transfers this policy to tertiary-structure targets to train a general co-design model, and finally applies frontier-guided fine-tuning to refine target-specific trade-offs among secondary-structure fidelity, tertiary-structure fidelity, and sequence diversity. Experiments show that TANGO generates diverse and novel designs, improving tertiary- and secondary-structure performance by 18.3% and 52.7%, respectively, over the strongest baseline.

Abstract
Designing RNA sequences that reliably fold into specific secondary structures is essential for understanding their biological functions but remains a challenging computational problem. We propose CocoRNA, a cooperative multi-agent reinforcement learning framework for RNA inverse design. CocoRNA simplifies the design task by decomposing it into smaller sub-problems, each solved collaboratively by multiple agents. This approach reduces the complexity of the problem and improves the exploration of design policies. During training, a centralized critic uses global structural information to guide the agents, enabling them to jointly optimize their design strategies. As a result, CocoRNA learns high-quality RNA design policies that generalize effectively to unseen structures without additional training. Experiments on the Rfam dataset demonstrate that CocoRNA substantially outperforms state-of-the-art methods in both success rate and design speed. Further experiments on other biological sequence design tasks highlight the effectiveness and broad potential of CocoRNA for complex design tasks.

Abstract

Abstract
Progress estimators play an essential role in providing dense reward signals for reinforcement learning in robotic manipulation, but accurately assessing task progress can be much challenging. Existing methods heavily rely on expert data and naively assume that all actions within a demonstration contribute to task success. This assumption often breaks down in real-world scenarios, especially in high-precision manipulation, where subtle geometric errors can lead to task failure. Therefore, these methods can erroneously interpret human mistakes as meaningful progress, further corrupting progress learning. We argue that a key limitation is the lack of a global prior regarding task evaluation criteria, which is crucial for distinguishing meaningful progress from human mistakes. To this end, we propose PRIME, a novel progress estimator capable of learning precise progress estimation from imprecise demonstrations by aggregating experience across trajectories. PRIME constructs a weighted graph over observed states, connecting states across trajectories based on spatial proximity and visual consistency. Edge weights encode spatial distances as transition costs, and shortest-path search approximates the geodesic distance to success. Changes in this distance naturally distinguish advancement, stagnation, and setbacks without assuming monotonic progress over time. Extensive experiments in both simulation and real world on precise manipulation tasks show that PRIME is consistently effective across diverse tasks, and robust to data with varying degrees of suboptimality. Notably, the advantage of PRIME gets more pronounced in higher-precision scenarios, yielding up to a \(5\times\) improvement in policy success rate over the SFT baselines on charger insertion.

Abstract
Language-model advice can accelerate reinforcement learning, but every call has a price, and a returned suggestion may be unparsable, unsafe to execute, or no better than the agent's own action. We introduce certified predictive advice gating, a cost-aware controller that forecasts possible advisor responses, values the action actually executed after parsing, execution checks, and cache reuse, and requests advice only when a calibrated lower bound on that value covers the price. Our analysis separates the gap of executed advice, cumulative regret under an intervention-stable fallback, and myopic acquisition loss. On BabyAI GoToObj with Qwen2.5-1.5B and 7B advisors, the gate improves return over no advice by paired $0.029\pm0.016$ and $0.030\pm0.015$ while using more than $97\%$ fewer calls than always asking. Matched-budget comparisons, however, do not resolve an advantage over an early query schedule, so the gain reflects economical advice volume rather than demonstrated state selection. Groupwise calibration raises coverage on positive-value opportunities from $0.47$ to $0.85$, still below the $0.90$ target. A separate headroom-aware gate reports paired gains over an early schedule of $0.032\pm0.012$ and $0.029\pm0.011$ on GoToObj and $0.027\pm0.011$ and $0.028\pm0.012$ on GoToObjDoor, and adds BabyAI and ALFWorld success, decision-stratum coverage, and component timing. These results separate return gains, query placement, and execution screening; finite-sample deployment guarantees remain unestablished.

Abstract
Conventional sleep staging provides a coarse-grained representation that obscures within-stage depth variation. We developed ECG-SDI, a single-lead electrocardiography (ECG) framework for continuous sleep-depth profiling and digital biomarker extraction. Using 12,392 overnight polysomnographic recordings, we trained and validated a two-stage deep learning model supervised by conventional sleep-stage labels. Abstract
Deep neural networks demonstrate remarkable generalization despite being highly over-parameterized, challenging traditional statistical learning theories. While several theoretical paradigms such as margin-based bounds, PAC-Bayesian analyses, and neural tangent kernels provide global explanations, they often lack mechanistic insight into how generalization emerges from training dynamics. We introduce Neural Expansion, a unified geometric framework that characterizes generalization through the expansion of directionally-selective ray coverage in the input space. Using Generalization Intervals (GIs), we quantify direction-specific regions around training samples where the model's predictions remain stable. Our extensive empirical studies across diverse architectures and datasets reveal that training leads to progressive expansion of ray coverage, particularly along directions with low Jacobian singular values. This mechanism helps explain a large fraction of correctly classified test inputs, adversarial examples, and even mislabeled or out-of-distribution samples through the geometry of ray coverage rather than instance-level memorization. By connecting input-space curvature to predictive behavior, our framework provides a mechanistic foundation that complements existing global theories of deep learning.

Abstract
Planning while moving faces an historical trade-off between deliberation and reactivity. A planner running concurrently with execution needs time to compute, so the robot must commit to an open-loop action chunk for that duration: a longer commitment buys a better plan at the cost of feedback frequency. Existing controllers fix this action commitment, and consequently the computation budget, before deployment. However, the optimal computation time is highly state-dependent (due to factors such as unmodeled dynamics, safety constraints, and marginal optimisation gains), i.e. dynamic, therefore offline static tuning risks leaving systems blind during critical maneuvers or unable to find global effective solutions. We cast the identification of the optimal computation budget as a semi-Markov decision process (SMDP) in which a single decision defines both the robot action commitments and the time allocated to the solver. To solve this, we train a reinforcement-learning policy conditioned on the current state, the executing plan, and inexpensive planner statistics. Abstract
Visible surface damage of banana fruits by Basilepta subcostata (the banana fruit scarring beetle) can reduce their market value. Here, manual inspection of Banana fruits is subjective and difficult to scale. This study presents a lightweight framework based on YOLOv8n deep learning model for locating individual bananas and classifying their visible surface condition as healthy or unhealthy. Abstract
Dexterous grasp synthesis has advanced rapidly in generating stable and physically plausible hand poses, but real-world manipulation requires grasps that preserve the function implied by the task. We study open-vocabulary task-oriented dexterous grasp generation, where a robot must infer functional intent from free-form language, ground it in multi-view visual observations and object geometry, and generate an executable high-degree-of-freedom grasp. We present OpenDexGrasp, a unified data and generative modeling framework for this setting. OpenDexVerse provides dual-source supervision organized by the Coverage-to-Alignment (C2A) Recipe: OpenDex-Scale offers large-scale semantic and geometric coverage through automatic grasp synthesis and vision-language annotation, while OpenDex-Align supplies high-quality embodied alignment through human teleoperation and category-level transfer. OpenDexGrasp learns a shared perception-action latent representation that couples open-vocabulary vision-language context with dexterous action generation. Affordance grounding and grasp generation provide complementary supervision over this latent space, enabling direct generation of task-consistent dexterous grasps without a separate affordance-to-pose inference stage. Extensive simulation and real-robot experiments demonstrate improved functional alignment, physical feasibility, generalization to unseen categories, and real-world execution success. Additional details and videos are available at https://opendexgrasp.github.io/.

Abstract
The Arcade Learning Environment (ALE) has been a cornerstone of Deep Reinforcement Learning research, providing a standardized benchmark for evaluating general agents. However, its lack of native support for object-extraction and environment modification restricts research into evaluating whether agents learn robust causal models or rely on brittle visual heuristics. Furthermore, the ALE's dependence on CPU-based emulation creates a significant bottleneck in modern GPU-accelerated training pipelines, limiting both experimental scale and iteration frequency. In this work, we introduce JAXtari, an open-source, high-performance reimplementation of Atari 2600 environments. Abstract
Obstacle avoidance for autonomous quadrotors in cluttered environments remains challenging, especially when both dense static obstacles and moving obstacles are present. Existing methods are often tailored to either static or dynamic settings, which always limit their adaptability across diverse environments. This paper presents TransNav, a transformer-based end-to-end navigation method for quadrotor obstacle avoidance in both static and dynamic environments. TransNav employs a patch-based point-cloud encoding scheme that combines local feature extraction with global contextual modeling, enabling compact yet informative environment representation. The method integrates perception and navigation in a single architecture and directly predicts a finite-horizon action sequence, from which the first action is executed. Moreover, we develop an unsupervised learning strategy by incorporating a lightweight point-mass dynamics model, which directly optimizes trajectory-level costs over the predicted sequence through numerical gradient propagation. Thus, the policy can be efficiently trained without expert demonstrations. Simulation and real-world experiments show that the proposed method achieves strong performance in dense static environments and competitive obstacle-avoidance capability in dynamic scenarios, while maintaining low inference cost for onboard deployment.

Abstract
Multi-class density estimators can count objects in crowded scenes, but their class-wise outputs offer no indication of how reliably density mass has been allocated. We introduce a training-free confidence proxy based on local competition between the strongest class channels. The resulting prominence measure requires only the predicted density maps, with no retraining or access to model internals. We test whether residual competition is diagnostic of cross-class leakage by attenuating contested density at inference time. On DSACA predictions for the Hicks et al. dataset, this reduces overall MAE from 0.920 to 0.846, suggesting that class competition contains useful information about count reliability.

Abstract
Time-aware retrieval is crucial for long-horizon LLM agents to effectively leverage ever-growing long-term memory. Existing methods focus on lexical or semantic similarity, leading to target memories being crowded out by temporally mismatched yet semantically similar candidates. Even time-aware approaches inject temporal cues only during memory organization or answer generation, overlooking temporal constraints at the retrieval stage, where candidate selection occurs - the root cause of the displacement. Inspired by the temporal contiguity effect in human episodic memory, we propose TIDER (Temporal Interval-driven Dual-channel Evidence Retrieval), a time-aware agentic retrieval framework. Central to TIDER is a reinforcement learning (RL)-trained temporal interval locator that infers the temporal windows in which the evidence lies. RL optimizes window localization as a discrete, non-differentiable retrieval decision, using composite rewards to convert sparse answer supervision into fine-grained retrieval feedback. These temporal windows first define the temporal retrieval channel; the retrieved candidates are then merged with results from the semantic channel and reranked by proximity to the windows. This temporal grounding enables TIDER to retrieve temporally aligned evidence that would otherwise be drowned out by the volume of semantically similar yet temporally mismatched memories. With a lightweight 1.7B locator, TIDER achieves state-of-the-art accuracy on both long-horizon memory benchmarks - 61.52% on LifeBench (+8.82%) and 78.96% on LoCoMo (+3.66%) - which indicates that temporal cues are crucial for pinpointing target evidence amid a vast pool of semantically similar historical events in long-term daily-life memory.

Abstract
Vision-language-action (VLA) models enable language-guided robot manipulation, but remain vulnerable to visual attacks that undermine user control over robot behavior. Prior studies optimize visual perturbations using fixed target supervision. However, the perturbation can change earlier actions and, in turn, the physical states reached later. As the attacked trajectory evolves, this supervision may no longer match the behavior needed at the states actually reached. Accurately matching these targets can therefore fail to advance the attacker-specified task. Even with suitable supervision, reducing average matching error does not ensure sustained task progress. The robot may still fail at a critical stage, and further updates can disrupt previously successful behavior. To address these limitations, we propose TRACO, a framework that enhances existing targeted visual attacks through closed-loop optimization. Its Execution-Driven Supervision rebuilds supervision for the states reached under attack to guide subsequent updates. Within this framework, Progress-Guided Selection focuses updates where progress stalls while maintaining supervision on the earlier behavior needed to reach that point. Experiments across VLA models and simulation environments show improved task-redirection success with TRACO, highlighting the security implications of closed-loop attack optimization.

Abstract
Many manipulation tasks that are effortless for dexterous human hands remain challenging for robotic systems equipped with simple, task-specialized grippers. Industrial platforms commonly rely on end-effectors such as suction cups or parallel-jaw grippers, yet no single mode can reliably satisfy the diverse constraints encountered across different phases of complex manipulation tasks, including pickup, reorientation, insertion, and constrained placement. In this work, we investigate whether complementary simple grippers within a heterogeneous bimanual setup can collectively realize tasks that exceed the capability of any individual gripper alone. Given a single human demonstration specifying the desired object motion, we formulate embodiment-aware execution as a finite-horizon optimization over arm-trajectory assignments. At each segment, the planner determines whether single-arm manipulation or a coordinated dual arm handover is required, jointly reasoning over heterogeneous grasp feasibility, motion constraints, manipulability, and cross-gripper compatibility. Exploiting this formulation, we compute globally consistent assignments with explicit switching regularization. Experiments on a dual Franka FR3 platform equipped with suction and parallel-jaw grippers demonstrate improved feasibility and reliability in tasks that are infeasible under single-gripper execution.
