Joints, Camera, Action! Co-Generating 3D Motion and Video for Arbitrary Rigs
Authors: Noam Rotstein, Yoad Tewel, Chen Tessler, Haggai Maron, Gal Chechik, Or Litany, Yoni Kasten, Yuval Atzmon
First: 2026-10-20T17:59:56+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-20T17:59:56+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2 · Project1
Abstract
Video generation models can now depict remarkably realistic motion. Yet it is unclear how to use this motion beyond pixels: to animate a specific 3D asset and replay the animation in its 3D scene. For instance, a video model can show a monkey jumping over a crossbar, but it cannot return that jump as replayable 3D motion placed in the 3D scene. Here we present a model that directly co-generates the video and 3D motion for any supplied rig, along with a camera that registers this motion to the scene. Prior video-motion models either handle only human skeletons, often adding billions of parameters, or triangulate 3D motion from generated 2D pose maps. We instead keep 3D motion in a separate model that supports arbitrary skeletons, and connect it to a pretrained video model through a small set of cross-attention layers. This separation lets the motion model first learn how skeletons move from motion-only data, which is easier to obtain and augment than paired video. Additionally, our model learns to locate each joint in the video frames, which lets us recover the camera and place the 3D motion in the scene. To train across many skeletons, we use a generative video editor to place animated 3D assets in scenes suited to their actions, curating the edits so their known motion remains valid. Together with human and robot video, this yields nearly 200K training pairs. Our evaluations demonstrate that our model animates diverse rigs from text more faithfully than video-first pipelines, and its motion responds to the surrounding scene. It also turns video into rig motion, rig motion into video, and enables keyframe-based control of the 3D motion. Videos and additional results are available on our project page: https://research.nvidia.com/labs/par/projects/jca/.

Learning Adaptable Dexterous Skills via Hybrid Dexterous Mimicking

Abstract
Dexterous multi-finger hands hold promise to unlock diverse skills like functional grasps, in-hand reorientation, and dynamic handover that the human-designed world assumes, yet existing learning recipes force a trade-off. Goal-reaching reinforcement learning generalizes to arbitrary object poses but, lacking any human prior, converges to unnatural and unreliable finger strategies; motion tracking reproduces human-like behavior from a single clip but cannot deviate from it. We present AdaDex, a goal-conditioned RL framework that turns a single hand-object trajectory of a few seconds---from mocap data or HOI reconstruction from in-the-wild RGB(D) video---into an adaptable dexterous skill. At the core of AdaDex is Hybrid Dexterous Mimicking: a shared policy is trained on two populations of massively parallel environments: motion tracking environments trained with dense motion tracking objective, which instill the human prior from human motion; and generalization environments with noised initial states and goals and a sparse object-success reward, which force the skill to work far off the reference. From seconds of human motion per skill, the resulting policies can learn diverse adaptable dexterous skills, such as functional grasping, object reposing, in-hand reorientation with finger gaiting, and dynamic behaviors like throw-and-catch. Our method outperforms baselines in terms of generalization and functionality, and we demonstrate transfer across simulators and to the real world. Project page: https://yuxuank.com/AdaDex/.

Authors: Rahul Sajnani, Diego Romeres, Mingyuan Zhang, Kai Gao, Carl Qi, Baichuan Huang, Srinath Sridhar, Dylan Randle, Lillian J. Ratliff, Jose Barreiros, Manikantan Nambi, Jeroen Vanbaar

Abstract
Motivated by the human ability to integrate experience across language, perception, and action, we introduce UniWALM, a unified world-action-language model that learns video, action, and language in a single shared representation, where each modality is both a conditioning signal and a supervision target. We ask: can jointly generating language enrich this shared representation, improving manipulation and supporting memory-dependent behavior? Unlike prior approaches that attach a reasoning or vision-language model to a video-action backbone and run them as distinct stages, UniWALM unifies all three modalities inside a single mixture-of-transformers backbone whose per-modality diffusion heads co-denoise synchronously, decoding subtask and textual memory in the same pass as action with no separate planning stage; the generated language is re-consumed on the next step, closing a language-as-memory loop without an external planner or memory bank. To keep the action stream reliable under this richer objective, we further introduce Flow Matching with Correction (FMC), a training objective that teaches recovery from perturbed action trajectories at no inference-time cost. UniWALM consistently improves over same-backbone baselines (e.g., FastWAM) on compositional pick-and-place (MolmoSpaces) and across the memory-dependent subset of RMBench, where it reaches 34.5% without any pretrained language or reasoning model and exceeds Mem-0's keyframe-equipped setup (28.5%) using only a single anchor frame. On a real-world Find-the-Item task, generating textual memory lifts success from 11.1% to 61.1%, while state-of-the-art VLAs stay at or below chance (<=33.3%).

Industrial ReMEmbR: Scaling Spatiotemporal Memory to Robot Fleets
Authors: Jad Yammine, Malek Wahidi, Abrar Anwar, Yan Chang, Mihir Acharya, Anthony Rizk
First: 2026-10-20T17:58:32+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-20T17:58:32+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Warehouse robot fleets continuously observe operational conditions, but preserving these observations as reusable semantic memory incurs substantial computation, communication, and storage costs. For example, explaining recurring aisle blockages can require reviewing video from multiple robots across several shifts. We present IndustrialRemembr, a shared spatiotemporal memory system that extends NVIDIA ReMEmbR to robot fleets and reduces server-side computation by generating scene descriptions only on demand. On each robot, an NVIDIA Jetson encodes video clips into retrieval embeddings with Cosmos-Embed1, vision tokens with Cosmos3-Edge, and reconstruction latents with the Wan2.2 VAE, without uploading raw camera frames. The encoding models are re-used to detect people in image patches, zeroing them out to support PII abstraction for GDPR-compliant deployments. We split Cosmos3-Edge at its vision-language boundary, placing the vision encoder on the robot and the language decoder on an on-premises server. A Nemotron agent retrieves memories by content, position, and time and queries the decoder using cached vision tokens. This design eliminates caption generation at write time, while cached-token decoding reproduces the unsplit model's output in all 450 tested decodes.

Authors: Xuanyu Lu, Fengqing Jiang, Kaiyuan Zheng, Yichen Feng, Yaorui Ding, Yuetai Li, Zhen Xiang, Bhaskar Ramasubramanian, Basel Alomair, Luyao Niu, Radha Poovendran

Abstract
World models learn to predict how their environment will evolve, making them an important foundation for general-purpose robotic control. Yet world action models depend on camera inputs whose manipulation can corrupt the visual representations used across tasks and action policies. Existing attacks on these models optimize against the victim's actions or predicted futures and therefore require access to target-model outputs. In this paper, we propose an attack, TAPDreamer, against world action models that instead uses a public encoder alone to construct a fixed local perturbation that transfers across tasks and action architectures. TAPDreamer requires no target-policy queries. Our key insight is that interactions between patch-induced changes in attention weights and value vectors broadcast a nearly identical representation shift far beyond the patch footprint, and this shift remains stable across task observations. Guided by this insight, TAPDreamer uses six frames from one source task to maximize the global L1 distance between clean and patched encoder representations. Abstract

Abstract
Continuous-time reinforcement learning seeks an optimal policy under unknown dynamics driven by stochastic differential equations using observations collected at discrete times. We develop a policy gradient framework that combines rigorous convergence analysis with learning from discrete data. Stochastic gradient descent (SGD) is the cornerstone of modern deep learning, yet its theoretical convergence properties remain poorly understood in non-convex settings. To address this gap, we study SGD dynamics in deep linear neural networks - simplified models that retain key non-convex characteristics while remaining analytically tractable. Building on recent results for gradient descent, we extend convergence guarantees to SGD under regularized squared loss. We establish almost sure boundedness of SGD iterates. Using the framework of asymptotic pseudotrajectories from stochastic approximation theory, our analysis establishes then, for both deterministic and adaptive step-size choices, convergence of SGD to the set of critical points under mild conditions (without relying on Lipschitz properties or whitened data). To our knowledge, this is the first work providing almost sure convergence guarantees for SGD in deep linear networks, bridging the gap between theoretical optimization and practical stochastic training.

Abstract

Abstract

Abstract
Humanoid-human interaction requires robots to operate under severe partial observability: during close physical contact, the human partner is occluded, truncated, or outside the robot's field of view, causing pose estimation to fail and often necessitating external tracking or privileged human state. At the same time, heavy mutual occlusions introduce errors into the human-human motion-capture data used for learning. We present REACT, a framework for learning deployable humanoid-human interaction policies from human-human demonstrations. REACT treats semantic contact structure as the key supervisory signal connecting demonstration processing, interaction learning, and onboard control. First, a VLM-guided contact modeling and retargeting pipeline identifies semantically meaningful contacts and transfers them onto humanoid-human geometry while preserving interaction structure. Using these annotations, privileged interaction policies learn physically grounded behaviors through interaction-aware contact rewards and are then distilled into deployable egocentric depth policies trained entirely from onboard sensing using photorealistic human depth rendering. To address severe partial observability, we introduce generative partner-state modeling objectives that predict partner motion from occluded egocentric views. We evaluate REACT in simulation and on a real Unitree G1 humanoid across five contact-rich interactions. Semantic contact modeling substantially improves preservation of demonstrated interactions, while generative partner-state modeling improves contact recall on four of five interactions, most under heavy occlusion. On real hardware, REACT performs all five interactions using onboard sensing alone.

Abstract
Laboratory measurements are essential for clinical decision making but typically require invasive blood sampling. Recent research suggests that ECG recordings may contain information beyond cardiac rhythm and provide information related to laboratory values. This thesis investigates NT-proBNP prediction from ECG recordings and patient metadata using a multimodal deep learning framework, while considering the physiological plausibility of ECG-based prediction. It also compares a 1-lead configuration using lead II with the corresponding 12-lead configuration. Additional experiments on glucose, hemoglobin, and potassium are presented in the appendix. Regression, binary classification, and three-class classification experiments were conducted using MIMIC-IV, with the three-class task based on clinically established thresholds. The 12-lead configuration consistently achieved better performance. Regression reached a mean absolute error of 534.98 pg/mL for 1-lead and 514.27 pg/mL for 12-lead. Binary classification achieved AUROCs of 0.849 and 0.876, respectively, while three-class classification achieved macro-averaged AUROCs of 0.738 and 0.752. The results indicate that precise NT-proBNP regression remains challenging, whereas broader concentration ranges can be classified more effectively. The findings are consistent with the physiological hypothesis that ECG characteristics and NT-proBNP concentrations reflect shared cardiovascular processes. ECG-based NT-proBNP prediction may therefore provide complementary information, particularly for classifying broader concentration ranges. However, laboratory measurements remain necessary, and validation on independent datasets is required.

Abstract
Tracking large human-motion datasets has become the dominant route to general, expressive humanoid whole-body control, but scaling across many motions is expensive. Current pipelines use model-free reinforcement learning, either merging per-motion specialists through lossy distillation, or brute-forcing a single policy with cluster-scale compute. SuperMimic amortizes learning across all motions with one differentiable world model of their shared dynamics, fit from logged transitions of a standard rigid-body simulator. The world model and the tracking policy train together in one loop, and backpropagation-through-time over short imagined rollouts gives the policy analytic gradients. Fast imagined rollouts and analytic gradients make policy learning efficient. In 3 hours on a single GPU, SuperMimic surpasses the return of a state-of-the-art motion tracker baseline trained for 7 days on 128 GPUs. It reduces GPU-hours by more than $1000\times$ and fails on only 25 of 129,514 benchmark motions, versus 179 failures for the baseline. After 9.8 hours on two GPUs, SuperMimic achieves zero failures. From 1 to 264 hours of motion, the world model's prediction error stays stable, and GPU-hours to a target return grow slowly. Our policy transfers to a physical Unitree G1 across diverse and acrobatic motions. SuperMimic removes compute as the bottleneck to scaling humanoid whole-body control, making it practical to measure motion-tracking scaling laws.

Abstract

Abstract
Generalizing manipulation policies across robot embodiments remains difficult because standard policies tie the interactions needed for a task to the actions of a particular robot. We study zero-shot cross-embodiment manipulation, where a policy trained on source embodiments must be deployed on a structurally different target embodiment without additional task demonstrations. We introduce Kinematic Interaction Transfer across Embodiments (KITE), which decouples manipulation into embodiment-agnostic interaction prediction and embodiment-specific kinematic decoding, connected through a learned representation of interaction intent based on contact patterns. An interaction policy is trained to predict the intent using demonstration data only collected on a source robot, and shared across morphologically different target robots. A kinematic decoder realizes these intents by learning from synthetic data generated from each embodiment's kinematic model. Through such decoupling, KITE can robustly transfer learned skills to a new embodiment given only its kinematic model, without recollecting any task-specific demonstration data. We evaluate KITE on four manipulation tasks in simulation and the real world spanning transfer between parallel grippers, dexterous hands, and composite embodiments. Abstract
Spiking neural networks target energy-efficient language modeling through sparse, binary activations, but the efficiency-accuracy tradeoff of spiking language models is uncharacterized at scale. We present the first Chinchilla-style IsoFLOP scaling study of a spiking language model, sweeping SpikeGPT-family models (RWKV backbone) from width 384 to 1536 across compute budgets from $3\times10^{17}$ to $10^{19}$ FLOPs on FineWeb-EDU. We report two findings. First, the compute-optimal frontier is $N_{opt} \propto C^{0.56}$, close to the 0.50 that Hoffmann et al. report for dense Transformers, so spiking does not change how parameters and data should be traded off under a compute budget. Second, against non-spiking RWKV and Transformer baselines trained the same way, the loss gap splits into a backbone cost of about 0.16 nats that does not change with compute and a spiking cost that falls steadily with compute, from 0.34 to 0.23 nats over our range. These results give the first compute-optimal recipe for spiking language models and quantify the accuracy cost of their sparsity.

Abstract
We present Kamino, a GPU-based physics solver for massively parallel simulations of heterogeneous highly-coupled mechanical systems. Implemented in Python using NVIDIA Warp and integrated into the Newton framework, it enables the application of data-driven methods, such as large-scale reinforcement learning, to complex robotic systems that exhibit strongly coupled kinematic and dynamic constraints such as kinematic loops. The latter are often circumvented by approximating the system's topology as a kinematic tree, and incorporating explicit loop-closure constraints or so-called mimic joints. Kamino aims at alleviating this burden by natively supporting these types of couplings. Moreover, Kamino supports heterogeneous worlds, allowing for batched simulation of structurally diverse robots on a single GPU. At its core lies a state-of-the-art constrained optimization algorithm based on ADMM, which computes constraint forces by solving the constrained rigid multi-body forward dynamics transcribed as a nonlinear complementarity problem. This leads to high-fidelity simulations that resolve contact dynamics without resorting to approximate models that simplify or convexify the problem. We demonstrate RL policy training on a biped with six nested kinematic loops, generating a feasible walking policy while simulating $4{,}096$ parallel environments on a single GPU.

Abstract
Deep learning models have become essential tools in medical imaging, yet training them end-to-end for diverse tasks remains challenging due to limited labeled data and computational constraints. To address this, we introduce Brain2Vec, a foundation model pre-trained on 74,425 T1-weighted Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) scans pooled from multiple studies. Additionally, its frozen embeddings outperform anatomically defined volumetric features on four of five downstream classification tasks and perform competitively with a state-of-the-art foundation model pre-trained on substantially more data. Agents built on vision language models (VLMs) now compete with learned policies on robot manipulation leaderboards. We ask how far a coding agent can go when it controls a robot instead of a computer, and where it stops. Our system, HexaShell, gives the agent a thin harness: one command line that turns its commands into robot motion and makes no decisions of its own. On the robot, the agent keeps doing what it does best: it reads output, writes code and runs tests. Two agents of the same model share the work. An acting agent runs each episode. After a failure, an optimizer agent reads the full record of the episode, with every command, image and message, finds the cause, and writes or fixes small programs called tools, each with its own tests. What the agents learn stays as code, and no demonstrations or training are needed. At run time, the tools use only what a real robot also has: RGB images, camera calibration and the state of the arms. They find objects by triangulation between camera views. Because these inputs are the same in simulation and on hardware, the tools move to a real robot with a different arm after only a small change for the arm. We build the tools on ten layouts of one seed per task with Codex and GPT-6-Astra and freeze them; the eight Open tasks share one set of tools. In RoboDojo's official evaluation on a hidden seed, 50 episodes for each of the 42 tasks, HexaShell reaches 46.9% success and a progress score of 53.2, the state of the art on the official RoboDojo leaderboard. The limit is contact, not reasoning: memory tasks reach 81.0%, but precision tasks only 32.0%. We open source the tools evolved so far at https://github.com/HexaFuture/HexaShell.
