Modeling and Understanding Neural Weights of Humanoid Policies with Diffusion
Authors: Huixuan Cheng, Bike Zhang, Shankar Sastry, Koushil Sreenath
First: 2026-10-14T17:59:52+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-14T17:59:52+00:00
Comments: Project page: https://humanoid-weight-diffusion.github.io/
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2 · Project1
Abstract
Recent advances in robot learning have driven rapid progress in humanoid control, enabling increasingly agile behaviors in the real world. However, humanoid control networks are typically obtained through conventional training procedures, while comparatively little attention has been given to directly modeling the distribution of their learned weights and generating functional networks in weight space. In this work, we investigate whether high-performing humanoid control networks can be generatively modeled directly through their weights. We adopt a latent diffusion framework that couples a linear autoencoder with a Diffusion Transformer. The autoencoder compresses actor network parameters into a compact latent space, and the Diffusion Transformer models the distribution of these latents. Trained on networks collected from reinforcement learning for the Unitree G1 robot, our approach generates deployable networks that perform various motions in the real world, including walking, dancing, and spin kick. Beyond generation, we further study the learned weight space to better understand the structure captured by these humanoid control networks. We identify a structural principle in the first-layer weights that consistently reflects the robot's kinematic prior. Notably, this kinematic prior emerges under both Reinforcement Learning (RL) and Imitation Learning (IL) training, suggesting that it is not specific to a particular optimization process but may instead reflect common knowledge associated with humanoid embodiment. Together, these results demonstrate the feasibility of generative modeling in humanoid network weight space, while providing initial insights into the information encoded by their learned weights. Additional results are available on our project website: https://humanoid-weight-diffusion.github.io/

Hierarchical Residual Policy Learning for Real-World Mobile Manipulation with Sparse Human Guidance
Authors: Zhefei Gong, Hefeifei Jiang, Zehao Wang, Jiachen Li

Abstract
Mobile Manipulation (MoMa) is a cornerstone of autonomous robotic systems, yet real-world fine-tuning remains a formidable challenge because of high-dimensional action spaces and intricate coupling between heterogeneous modules. In this paper, we propose HiRe-MoMa (Hierarchical Residual Mobile Manipulation), a systematic framework for stable and sample-efficient real-world fine-tuning of complex MoMa systems. At its core, HiRe-MoMa adopts a Hierarchical Residual Architecture that fine-tunes a frozen base policy. A Policy-Agnostic Residual learns additive corrections atop the base policy, while Hierarchical Decoupling splits the residual into mobile-base and manipulator-arm heads with unidirectional conditioning, mitigating gradient interference and aligning corrections with the platform's physical hierarchy. To improve sample efficiency in data-expensive real-world settings, we propose Expert-Guided Value Alignment, which leverages sparse human interventions with under 20% training coverage to reshape the critic's value landscape, effectively guiding autonomous exploration through expert priors. Extensive real-world experiments on the TIAGo++ platform demonstrate that HiRe-MoMa consistently enhances task performance with only around 30 minutes of online interaction, regardless of the base policy architecture. To our knowledge, this is among the first frameworks to enable efficient on-robot fine-tuning for mobile manipulation in the real world, providing a practical paradigm for deploying complex robotic systems in dynamic, human-centric environments. Further details can be found on our project website: https://hire-moma.github.io/

Abstract
Training language agents is bottlenecked by the high cost of long-horizon rollouts, yet standard agentic reinforcement learning (RL) learns only from a terminal scalar reward, leaving the supervision in intermediate steps largely unused. Prior work exploits this supervision through value targets that estimate per-step success probabilities or observation targets that fit environment response. Both are hard to construct well: (1) value targets come from the same single scalar signal for each step in the trajectory, and (2) observation targets, such as web pages and terminal logs, are long and noisy and reach only one step ahead. We propose \name, which adds a third kind of multi-step action targets. On successful on-policy trajectories, \name trains the policy backbone to generate the action sequences that follows each history. Each action is also supervised when forecast from the preceding histories, forming a temporal ensemble that the shared backbone absorbs during training, and this enables acting and forecasting to co-evolve. The policy still acts one step at a time at inference, with no overhead. Across five benchmarks (ALFWorld, ScienceWorld, WebShop, $τ^2$-bench, and AppWorld), \name (1) achieves stronger aggregate performance than GRPO and RL baselines with predictive auxiliary targets across simulated environments, while improving overall performance on both realistic applications; (2) delivers up to $1.9\times$ and $2.0\times$ higher sample efficiency than ECHO and GRPO, respectively, on ScienceWorld; and (3) achieves these gains with a single policy backbone, without additional models, separate pretraining, or inference overhead. The codebase is available at https://github.com/ulab-uiuc/CoPE.

Abstract
Computer-use agents have demonstrated strong capabilities in interpreting screens and executing keyboard and mouse actions. However, games, with their visually rich worlds and diverse mechanics, remain challenging and offer a demanding next step toward general digital control. Current game-agent learning pipelines often rely on large-scale demonstrations with limited coverage of dynamic game states, while scalable environments for interactive training remain difficult to obtain. We introduce Player-1, a post-training framework for learning game control beyond demonstrations through synthetic experience. We extend the computer-use action space with relative pointer motion, sustained inputs, and composite actions. For supervised initialization, we fine-tune computer-use models on 6,044 curated gameplay trajectories that pair recorded actions with task instructions and visual reasoning. We construct Player-1-Gym, sixteen synthetic 2D and 3D environments designed around control demands shared across games. Programmatically verifiable rewards and randomized observations and control dynamics support multi-environment reinforcement learning with PPO. The resulting Player-1-9B and Player-1-35B-A3B improve MineDojo-300 success over their SFT policies by 27.8 and 17.7 percentage points, respectively. Player-1-9B reaches 38.33% success compared with 12.63% for the strongest evaluated baseline, with further gains over SFT on ViZDoom-10 and GameWorld. Further analyses show that broader synthetic environment coverage improves transfer, while the use of composite actions that combine multiple keyboard and mouse inputs increases during RL. We demonstrate that learning from synthetic experience substantially improves game control beyond supervised initialization, with gains that generalize beyond the RL training suite.

PingpongDuo: Agile Humanoid Table Tennis Doubles with Safe Team Coordination
Authors: Haoran Jiang, Yichao Zhong, Kai Zhang, Cheng Liu, Yixuan Pan, Zhenjie Yang, Jingbo Wang, Tianyu Li, Li Chen

First: 2026-10-14T17:56:27+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-14T17:56:27+00:00

Abstract
Unlike singles, humanoid table tennis doubles requires teammates to strike in strict alternation while sharing a confined workspace. Rapid repositioning can compromise striking accuracy, while uncoordinated teammate movements introduce collision risks. We present PingpongDuo, a hierarchical framework for agile humanoid doubles with safe team coordination. At the team level, a planner jointly selects positioning targets and uses learned risk estimates to filter risky proposals, preferentially preserving the current striker's target. At the individual level, separate footwork and striking skills combine agile movement with precise racket control. The skills are guided by human motion references, including footwork collected from human doubles play, and trained from states produced by one another to support rapid transitions. A racket-arm residual further refines strikes after movement using return feedback from a simulator calibrated with real ball-flight and contact measurements. In simulation, PingpongDuo improves the ball-return success rate by 13.2% over the state-of-the-art baseline, while achieving approximately 2 cm positioning error during clearance. In real-world experiments, a robot completes a 2.15 m clearance-and-recovery cycle in 2.4 s with a peak speed of 2.23 m/s, while two robots sustain more than 10 consecutive strikes in strict alternation.

Abstract
Post-training binds the competence of an LLM agent to the interface it was trained on. Semantics-preserving perturbations of that interface, such as renaming an action or changing the notation, leave the underlying task unchanged yet make the agent worse at it. Scoring each sample independently means interface randomization only broadens this dependence from a single interface to the training interface distribution, so out-of-distribution perturbations still degrade the agent. We propose Cross-Interface Policy Regularization (CIPR), a self-supervised objective that replaces action names with uninformative identifiers and presents each training sample under two interfaces in which equivalent actions are identified by construction. CIPR penalizes the KL divergence between the action distributions induced under the two interfaces, so the policy behaves consistently across them. We evaluate CIPR in both supervised and reinforcement-learning post-training, where it improves robustness. On agentic function calling, held-out interface perturbations reduce the accuracy of a post-trained agent by nearly 25%, and CIPR recovers 10.5% while leaving accuracy on the unchanged interface intact.

Abstract
Online reinforcement learning (RL) relies on the Markov property for guaranteed performance, but real-world applications often lack well-defined states given raw observed variables. While causal RL has attracted growing interest, existing work typically assumes Markovian states are provided and uses causality only to accelerate learning. This leaves a fundamental gap: \emph{given a longitudinal causal graph over observed variables, how does one construct MDP states that provably satisfy the Markov property?} We provide a procedure that constructs a provably minimal such state. In deep RL, the minimal representation alone empirically fails to improve performance, indicating that neural networks cannot automatically exploit Markovian minimality. We therefore propose \textbf{MOSE} (Multi-Order State Exposure), which exposes a single shared Q-function to history-based state constructions of orders 1 through $W$ simultaneously. An attention-based selection layer scores the dimensions of this combined input by their estimated relevance to value-function estimation and selects the high-scoring ones. Causal MOSE further constrains the selection layer to retain every variable of the minimal state, guaranteeing sufficiency while leaving the model free to admit useful redundancy. MOSE consistently outperforms both the minimal state construction and single-window policies on common benchmarks and synthetic datasets. Including the minimal representation alongside MOSE can further improve performance. Our results establish a core principle for causal deep RL: minimal sufficiency is not enough. \emph{Controlled redundancy} gives the model flexibility to learn its state representation end-to-end. Causal state information provides structural guidance that can make this learning more efficient.

Abstract
3D object composition is pivotal to digital content creation and robot simulation, while also serving as a critical testbed for physical intelligence in foundation models. Previous methods either leverage large language models to directly predict object coordinates, or utilize generative diffusion priors to synthesize composite images for visual alignment, both of which rely on static priors in digital spaces. However, these methods consistently struggle with complex compositions such as precise hanging, gravity-driven settling and hidden containment, as these tasks require interactive construction processes involving global planning, fine-grained alignment, and physical simulation. To address these challenges, we propose PhySGA, a Physics-Space Grounded Agentic Framework for 3D composition via agent exploration and physical simulation, enabling the universal interactive assembly of rigid, articulated, and deformable objects. Directly applying vanilla LLMs or VLMs to this task proves insufficient, as current models inherently lack grounded physical understanding and interactive construction experience. To overcome this limitation, we design a collaborative feedforward-feedback architecture: an Assembly Agent focuses on geometric and kinematic reasoning for global step planning and coarse adjustment, while a Critic Agent reasons over contact dynamics for simulator state diagnosis and fine-grained closed-loop correction. Furthermore, Action Routers map semantic planning and analysis onto executable physical primitives within the simulator, while a task-specific Self-Evolving Skill Library distills historical trial-and-error feedback into reusable experiences, boosting success rates and minimizing exploration attempts. Extensive experiments within Isaac Sim demonstrate that PhySGA significantly surpasses existing methods in relation fidelity, physical plausibility, and visual quality.

Abstract
Coding agents can construct executable programs and revise them through feedback, offering a foundation for embodied control without target-task fine-tuning. Abstract
Relational deep learning has emerged as a powerful paradigm for learning end-to-end predictive models over relational databases by representing them as temporal heterogeneous graphs. Recent advances based on graph neural networks and Transformers have achieved strong performance across diverse domains. Abstract
This paper rethinks steady-hand robotic manipulation by using a weakly supervised framework that fuses calibration-aware perception with admittance control. Unlike conventional automation that relies on labor-intensive 2D labeling, our framework leverages reusable warm-up trajectories to extract implicit spatial information, thereby achieving calibration-aware, depth-resolved perception without the need for external fiducials or frame-by-frame manual depth annotation. By explicitly characterizing residuals from observation and calibration models, the system establishes a task-space error budget from recorded warm-ups. Under the evaluated laboratory conditions, the recorded trajectories yielded an empirical lateral error level of approximately 49μm and a conservative axial error estimate of approximately 291 μm during large in-plane moves. In a within-subject user study (N=8), the learned agent reduces overall NASA-TLX workload by 77.1% relative to the simple steady-hand assistance baseline. Together, these results demonstrate successful closed-loop micromanipulation and reduced subjective workload under the evaluated laboratory conditions, without external fiducials or frame-by-frame manual depth annotation, supporting the proposed approach as a framework for microscope-guided robotic assistance.

Abstract
Long-horizon precise manipulation produces mixed-quality data for post-training vision-language-action (VLA) models: even successful demonstrations and intervention-assisted rollouts may contain detours, hesitations, failed attempts, and imperfect recoveries. The key challenge is to identify which action chunks actually advance the task, rather than uniformly cloning or discarding imperfect trajectories. We propose Graph-Reweighted Behavior Cloning (GR-BC), an offline iterative post-training framework that builds a task-conditioned goal-reachability graph over demonstrations and iteratively collected rollouts, including both successful and failed episodes with human interventions. Reverse graph search computes minimal remaining steps to task success within the dataset support, and each action chunk is weighted by how much its execution reduces this potential. This consequence-based weighting reuses recoverable segments from failed trajectories, leverages interventions as recovery paths, and down-weights locally regressive chunks even inside successful trajectories. New rollouts are incorporated by appending them to the graph and rerunning search, without training a reward model, action-conditioned critic, or progress predictor. Across four precise manipulation tasks and three VLA backbones, GR-BC improves single-round supervised fine-tuning and reaches 91% success after a few offline iterations, without trajectory comparisons or progress labels. Abstract
Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) trains language models from checked task outcomes, but assigning a terminal reward to individual reasoning tokens or actions remains difficult. Group-relative methods such as Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) sample several attempts per prompt, but lose their reward-based learning signal when all attempts receive the same outcome. We propose Hindsight-Privileged RLVR (HP-RLVR), a single-rollout method that turns a completed trajectory's own future into token-level update weights. The generating policy snapshot scores each eligible token under its causal history and under a hindsight context containing the realized future and verifier feedback. Abstract
Navigation for humanoid robots is critical, yet large-scale evaluation on physical hardware is often impractical due to cost and safety concerns, making simulation benchmarks essential. Existing VLN benchmarks achieve physically executable navigation, but still assume (1) all hazards are observable from elevation maps; (2) realized motions closely match desired motions. Abstract
Certifiable optimization provides global optimality guarantees for non-convex state estimation in robotics. However, under severe physical sensing degeneracy, convex Semidefinite Programming relaxations solved via Interior-Point Methods suffer from rank expansion, rendering direct semidefinite solving and spectral rounding algebraically invalid. In this paper, we establish a universal candidate-driven dual certification framework that resolves this challenge without incurring rank expansion. First, from an epigraph perspective, we prove that while sensing degeneracy expands the primal optimal set into a flat equi-cost face, cost tightness and multiplier recovery remain strictly intact, enabling global verification without direct semidefinite solving. Second, we propose an observability-aware local search using solution remapping, coupled with a closed-form dual multiplier recovery scheme that executes in sub-microsecond latency. Third, we derive a model-specific adaptive dual safety threshold parameterized by the minimum non-zero eigenvalue of the observable Hessian and uneradicated residual leakage, preventing false rejections of optimal candidates under weak observability. We validate our universal framework with robust registration under degenerate measurements.

Abstract
In reinforcement learning, an agent must explore an unknown environment to learn how to make good decisions. Unlike methods that can freely sample any state--action pair, an agent learning from a single trajectory must spend additional time navigating between states. It is therefore natural to expect that learning from a single trajectory requires more experience. In this paper, we show that this additional cost is not always necessary. For average-reward reinforcement learning, we develop an algorithm that learns where to collect information while ensuring that the required samples can be obtained efficiently. We establish an upper bound on the number of interactions needed to identify a near-optimal policy. Furthermore, we prove a matching information theoretic lower bound, showing that, for a broad class of environments and at sufficiently high accuracy, learning from a single trajectory is no harder in the worst case than learning with unrestricted access to state action samples. Our results reveal that the additional complexity cost of navigation can be avoided by adapting information gathering requirements to how the environment provides information.

Abstract
Real-time volumetric mapping for a planning robot is dominated by the cost of integrating and storing information at the finest resolution, yet only a small fraction of any map ever lies on or near a path the planner actually considers. We exploit this with a tiered-resolution mapping mechanism that integrates LiDAR into a single map structure at two resolutions simultaneously: fine inside a corridor around the robot's current plan, and coarse everywhere else. As opposed to traditional navigation pipelines where path planning uses the mapping result and provides no feedback, we propose a feedback loop where planning also informs mapping to dynamically integrate information at different leaf resolutions. Across two real-world teleoperated datasets, replayed and scored offline against an independent full-resolution map, the tiered maps are 13-15x smaller and require 6-8x less integration CPU time than a uniform fine map, while producing paths at a similar cost per meter as the ones generated from fine resolution maps on both scenarios. A uniform coarse map reaches the same size savings but its path quality collapses (+210% cost per meter) on the harder scenario, because it over-discretizes terrain the robot must traverse. Tiering approximates the coarse map's cheapness without its failure mode.

Abstract
Safely executing long-horizon multi-arm tasks online in complex dynamic environments remains challenging. We propose a new distributed dynamic Gaussian belief propagation (DD-GBP) method that directly plans joint-space trajectories through parallel local inference. A unified Gaussian-process (GP) parameterization connects adaptive planning horizons, interpolation-time factors, and continuous execution. Each robot maintains a local tree-structured factor graph and exchanges messages asynchronously over active inter-arm links. Dynamic subnet reconstruction and event-triggered updates reduce communication and inference costs while responding to moving obstacles. We also provide theoretical analyses of local inference accuracy, anytime early stopping, triggered-replanning consistency, and GP-consistent execution. In 50 long-horizon, multi-target four-arm scenes with moving obstacles, DD-GBP achieves $92.5\%$ collision-free all-target completion and the highest success rate among six representative baselines. Scenario ablations demonstrate long-horizon safety and robustness to communication impairment and extreme conditions; success remains $86.0\%$ under $90\%$ packet loss. Execution results further show substantially suppressed extreme velocity and acceleration peaks relative to Syn-GBP. On one workstation, obstacle-free tests with 30 coexisting arms retain $99.87\%$ success and a $15.80$ ms mean warm solve, exceeding 60 Hz and outperforming the compared methods in scalability. Dual-arm transport experiments under dynamic interference demonstrate online response and practical deployability. To the best of our knowledge, these results establish a new state of the art for online long-horizon multi-arm planning in dynamic scenes.

Abstract
Learning-based models for radio resource management (RRM) are typically built for a single function and deployment, so each new setting repeats the development pipeline. RRM decisions, however, share a common structure: each is assembled from interdependent fields, defined by the standard, whose values are selected in view of the network state. We propose RRM-GPT, an autoregressive framework for RRM foundation models that generate these decisions as a language model generates text. An encoder maps heterogeneous network observations into a common token representation, and a decoder emits the decision one field at a time, each conditioned on the network state and the fields already committed. Pretraining on unannotated network logs teaches the model what makes a decision valid and how controllers choose among valid decisions; post-training then adapts it to deployment-specific operator objectives through imitation or reinforcement learning. The framework targets two forms of reuse: a function-specific model reused across deployments, and a model shared across RRM functions that generates their interdependent decisions as one sequence. In a 5G New Radio (NR) case study, we demonstrate that a single model generates complete scheduling grants spanning user selection, timing, link adaptation, resource allocation, and control signaling. The model captures dependencies among grant fields and transfers learned behavior to an unseen scenario without adaptation.

Abstract
Contact-rich manipulation requires robots to coordinate not only where to move, but also how contact and force should evolve throughout an interaction. Yet most robot policies represent actions primarily as motion trajectories, using tactile signals as inputs or auxiliary prediction targets without explicitly encoding contact dynamics in the action representation. We propose ILA (Interaction Latents as Actions), which uses decodable interaction latents that jointly capture the evolution of motion, contact, and force application as policy action units, providing a shared representation for action generation, evaluation, and offline improvement. We learn these latents through visually conditioned joint action-tactile reconstruction with a time-conditioned implicit decoder. Using them as flow-matching generation targets supports post-training of pretrained vision-language-action models (VLAs) and world action models (WAMs), as well as training flow-matching policies from scratch. A shared decoder maps generated latents to executable actions. For offline improvement, we learn a tactile-conditioned expert reference model from interaction latents encoded from expert demonstrations. The resulting expert-consistency rewards guide weighted learning from successful rollouts, while observation-compatible expert references guide correction of low-reward predictions from failed rollouts. Across three policy families, ILA improves success rates averaged over four real-world tasks by 21.0-36.5 percentage points relative to tactile-conditioned direct-action baselines. Ablations highlight the benefit of encoding contact evolution in action targets beyond tactile conditioning, while the same latent space supports expert-guided offline improvement. Abstract

Abstract
Though modern AI models like LLMs predict discrete tokens, today's robotic foundation models represent actions as continuous vectors. In this paper, we challenge this common consensus by asking if continuous action parametrizations are superior to their discrete counterparts. We conduct an extensive comparison of single-step regression and multi-step flow variants of both continuous and discrete policies in both from-scratch single-task and multi-task VLA training, in both simulation and on real hardware. In all regimes, we find that discrete action prediction, even the most naive, uniform-bin quantization, can match or even outperform continuous-action policies in task success rate. Moreover, we find that the challenge to discrete action policies is \emph{not} action precision, but rather factorization error introduced by product-distribution predictions across time and action dimensions. We show that, when paired with the correct interventions---iterative computation through discrete flows, or distribution sharpening through classifier-free guidance---discrete flow policies can turn this weakness into a strength, enabling more responsive action changes during a predicted action chunk. Lastly, we show that attempts to explicitly inject geometric information into discrete policies, e.g. through corruption processes that emulate continuous flow matching, do \emph{not} yield consistent improvements. Taken together, our findings question existing assumptions that robotic action prediction requires distinct design decisions compared to other AI domains, and motivates the possibility of discrete action prediction as a compelling alternative to today's continuous action parametrizations. Abstract
Momentum is a classical tool for accelerating convex optimization and a standard component of modern large language model training. Yet recent empirical work has questioned the usefulness of first-moment momentum in reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR). We study this question through a bandit formulation of RLVR with bias-corrected exponential moving average (EMA) momentum, where the EMA coefficient $β$ controls how quickly the weights on past gradients decay. When policy gradients are evaluated exactly, we prove that increasing $β$ within a range that prevents overshoot strictly accelerates optimization. When gradients are estimated from sampled responses, a positive expected gain can be obscured by sampling fluctuations. We show that as the learning rate $η$ decreases, the expected gain with fixed $β$ vanishes faster than these fluctuations, whereas increasing $β$ to keep $η/(1-β)$ within the safe regime preserves a nonvanishing gain that eventually dominates them. Bandit simulations quantitatively validate these predictions. RLVR experiments on Qwen3-4B-Base using SGD with EMA and AdamW support our principle of choosing larger coefficients in practical LLM post-training. 动量是加速凸优化的经典工具，也是现代大语言模型训练的标准组件。然而，近期实证工作对一阶动量在可验证奖励的强化学习（RLVR）中的有效性提出了质疑。我们通过带偏差校正指数移动平均（EMA）动量的 RLVR 赌博机形式研究这一问题，其中 EMA 系数 $β$ 控制过去梯度权重的衰减速度。当策略梯度被精确评估时，我们证明在防止过冲的范围内增大 $β$ 会严格加速优化。当梯度由采样响应估计时，正期望增益可能被采样波动掩盖。我们表明，随着学习率 $η$ 减小，固定 $β$ 下的期望增益比这些波动消失得更快，而增大 $β$ 使 $η/(1-β)$ 保持在安全范围内，则能保留最终主导波动的非消失增益。赌博机模拟定量验证了这些预测。在 Qwen3-4B-Base 上使用带 EMA 的 SGD 和 AdamW 进行的 RLVR 实验支持我们在实际 LLM 后训练中选择更大系数的原则。在 Qwen2.5-32B 上使用 AdamW 时，仅增大 $β_1$ 就使平均验证准确率比传统选择提高了近 $2$ 个百分点，证明了该原则在更大模型规模上的有效性。
Summary / 总结
This paper investigates when first-moment momentum actually helps reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR), since recent empirical work has questioned its usefulness despite momentum being standard in large language model training. The authors analyze a bandit formulation of RLVR with bias-corrected exponential moving average momentum, where the coefficient beta controls the decay of past gradient weights, and they derive theoretical results for both exact and sampled policy gradients. They prove that with exact gradients, increasing beta within a range that avoids overshoot strictly accelerates optimization, while with sampled gradients the expected gain can be masked by sampling noise; as the learning rate decreases, the gain from fixed beta vanishes faster than the fluctuations, but raising beta to keep the ratio of learning rate to one minus beta in a safe regime preserves a nonvanishing gain that eventually dominates. Bandit simulations quantitatively confirm these predictions, and RLVR experiments on Qwen3-4B-Base with SGD plus EMA and AdamW support choosing larger coefficients, with Qwen2.5-32B under AdamW showing that simply increasing beta-one improves mean validation accuracy by nearly two percentage points over the conventional choice.

Abstract
Accurate long-range weather forecasting remains a major challenge for AI models, both because errors accumulate over autoregressive rollouts and because reanalysis training datasets provide limited sampling of the slow modes of variability underpinning predictability. Most AI and all numerical weather prediction models are autoregressive, repeatedly applying short-lead forecasts to reach subseasonal-to-seasonal (S2S) or seasonal lead times, often causing instability and miscalibration. Long-timestep probabilistic models that generate long-range forecasts in a single step offer an alternative, but training on the 40-year reanalysis record leads to overfitting, suggesting orders of magnitude more data are required. We introduce long-range distillation, whereby a long-timestep probabilistic ``student'' model is trained to forecast directly at long-range using a huge synthetic dataset generated by an autoregressive ``teacher'' model. Using the Deep Learning Earth System Model (DLESyM) as teacher, we generate over 10,000 years of simulated climate to train student models across a range of timescales. In perfect-model experiments, the distilled models outperform climatology and approach their teacher's skill while replacing hundreds of autoregressive steps with a single timestep. In the real world, both a diffusion model student fine-tuned on ERA5 and a linear inverse model student with mixed DLESyM-ERA5 training achieve S2S forecast skill competitive with the ECMWF IFS ensemble, with synthetic pre-training providing improvements beyond ERA5-only training. This demonstrates the benefit of synthetic pre-training for both deep learning and classical data-driven methods, and represents the first demonstration that real-world skill can be improved by training on AI-generated synthetic data in volumes far exceeding the observational record.
